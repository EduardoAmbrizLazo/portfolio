Overview

For our final E80 project, my team built an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) that was supposed to measure the distance to an object it was following. Ideally, that distance would feed a control loop on the motor PWM so the vehicle could hold a constant following distance.

Instead of relying on a single sensor, we compared two very different ways of measuring distance:

  1. Ultrasonic time of flight with an HC-SR04, which sends out pulses and times the echo.
  2. Force and Hooke’s Law, where a force-sensing resistor (FSR) reads the force on a spring-loaded extrusion and a calibrated spring constant turns that force into a distance.

We also built an anemometer around a hall effect sensor to log wind speed during deployment, so we could explain any odd behavior in the distance data.

Then it rained at Dana Point. The ultrasonic sensor was not waterproof and failed, so the deployment turned into a test of the novel FSR-and-spring approach to object detection.

The Sensors

Force-Sensing Resistor and Spring

The idea comes straight from Hooke’s Law, \(F_s = k\,\Delta x\), so \(\Delta x = F_s / k\). If the FSR measures the force on a spring with a known spring constant \(k\), the spring’s compression tells us how far away an object is when the extrusion touches it. FSRs are normally used to detect pressure, so using one this way was a creative twist, and FSRs are also easy to calibrate with just a few known loads.

The FSR sits in a voltage divider with a 10 kΩ resistor:

\[V_{out} = \frac{R_g}{R_g + R_{p1}} V_{in}\]

An op-amp stage then adds gain and an offset so the signal lands in a 1–3 V range, comfortably inside the Teensy’s readable range.

We calibrated the sensor by applying known forces and measuring the output. A cubic polynomial fit the force-to-resistance relationship very well (R² = 0.999), and the force-to-voltage relationship through our actual circuit also fit a cubic (R² = 0.989), which is the one we used.

The last calibration was the spring itself, which relates the sensed force (and so the voltage) to a physical displacement. Running multiple trials gave an uncertainty on each distance value.

Ultrasonic Distance Sensor

The HC-SR04 estimates distance from how long an ultrasonic pulse takes to bounce back: distance = (high-level time × 340 m/s) / 2. It needs a trigger pulse, so the circuit uses an NA555P timer in astable configuration that fires the trigger roughly every 345 ms. The sensor’s echo output is then scaled with a non-inverting op-amp stage (gain of 4), so about 1 meter of range maps to 0–2.1 V.

We calibrated it by recording the average peak voltage over 10 trials at 10 cm increments from 10 cm to 1 m. A straight line fit well (R² = 0.993), with distance in cm ≈ 46.5 × V − 3.01.

A dry test on the AUV’s protoboard showed the setup only reached about 1.2 m, well short of the 4 m the datasheet promises.

Hall Effect Anemometer

To log the weather, we built a cup anemometer that carries a magnet. Each time the magnet passes the AH9246 hall effect sensor, the output drops, and counting those drops gives revolutions per second. A single OP07 op-amp conditions the output to 1–3 V.

The software side took the most work. We extended the base ADC loop so it watches for voltage drops, counts revolutions with an interrupt, and computes RPS over each 69 ms sampling window. Calibration happened in a wind tunnel in two steps: first relating the tunnel’s fan RPM to wind speed, then relating the anemometer’s RPS to that wind speed.

Mechanical Design

The frame had a lot of competing requirements. The motors had to be underwater, the anemometer had to be elevated to catch real wind, and the electronics box had to stay dry above the waterline.

  • Stability: a large surface area keeps the AUV from tipping, but adds weight up high. We kept the center of mass below the center of buoyancy so the vehicle self-rights, and added pool noodles for flotation.
  • Dry electronics: the box holding the Teensy, motherboard, and protoboard sits above the water for safety.
  • Anemometer frame: the cups spin a magnet past the hall sensor. Mounting that assembly tilted the vehicle’s balance, which is why the other two sensors got their own second frame.
  • Spring and extrusion frame: a CAD-designed housing holds a spring-loaded extrusion about 14.5 inches long that changes length as force is applied, and an elevated panel gives the ultrasonic sensor something to reflect off.

Deployment

We deployed the AUV at the shore of Baby Beach in Dana Point, California, to keep the vehicle easy to recover and minimize any environmental impact. A previous lake deployment nearly lost a piece of the vehicle, so staying close to shore was deliberate. We also checked the onboard circuitry before and after the run.

Each run went like this:

  1. Insert an SD card into the motherboard and flip the power switch.
  2. Close the circuitry into its harnessed box and strap it to the AUV.
  3. Let the motors drive the AUV forward until the FSR feels a high enough force (the extrusion pressed against a team member), then back off and repeat.
  4. Retrieve the AUV, then read the SD card log in MATLAB with logreader.m to plot each sensor’s raw voltage. Wind speed was also watched live in the Arduino Serial Monitor.

What Went Wrong

The day of deployment it rained, and the HC-SR04 was not waterproof. The mechanical design had accounted for water, but we didn’t adequately consider the effect of rain on the electronics. The sensor failed, and the resulting short also affected the hall effect sensor. Fortunately we found a replacement for the hall sensor, but the ultrasonic sensor was lost.

Results

  • Ultrasonic sensor: no usable data. Despite repeated attempts to trigger it with a panel in close proximity, its output stayed constant after the rain damage.
  • Anemometer: worked as expected. Live readings ranged from about 2 m/s to 6.21 m/s, in line with the day’s reported average wind of 14 mph (about 6.25 m/s). The remaining discrepancy was likely due to the anemometer’s low elevation.
  • FSR and spring: the main result. Within the pre-specified distance during deployment, the FSR output swung between 1 and 3 V for forces of roughly 0.36 N, 0.853 N, and 1.36 N, which supports the force-to-distance idea.

We compared two trials with different motor power: Deployment 1 at 120 PWM and Deployment 2 at 290 PWM.

In Deployment 1, the FSR looked nearly digital instead of showing the slow rise in force our dry tests produced. Comparing against the video showed the FSR’s response was valid, but the gradual change was missing from the data. The most likely cause is aliasing: at a 14.4 Hz sampling rate, the force can change faster than we sample it.

What I’d Do Differently

  • Waterproof the ultrasonic sensor and rework its output into an analog voltage proportional to distance, so it can drive a real control loop on motor PWM, the same way the anemometer software works.
  • Raise the sampling rate to avoid aliasing and make post-processing easier.
  • Elevate the anemometer more so it sees real wind speeds.
  • Recalibrate any replacement sensors before deployment.

This project showed how much each sensor demands in its own way: mechanical design for the spring and frame, software for the anemometer, and careful circuit work for the ultrasonic sensor.
