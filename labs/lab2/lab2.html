Multiplexed Seven Segment Display

Lab Report
Author

Eduardo Ambriz Lazo

Published

September 15, 2026

Introduction

In this lab, we built upon the foundation of a single seven segment display leveraged the FPGA aboard the MicroP’s Dev Board to branch out to a dual seven segment display which was integrated using multiplexing logic to properly select a DIP switch as an input the continuously flash the 2 hex digits of the display fast enough to be undetectable to the human eye. The decoding module and counter module are identical to Lab 1 save for a new top-level test bench that makes use of the parameterized counter to flash and assign logic such that the right DIP switch and digit (i.e. common anode on the dual seven segment display) is selected. The multiplexing logic allows us to control 2 digits with just 9 pins and utilizing the counter module, we aim for the internal 48 MHz clock to be divided by ~18’b0 resulting in a clock at ~90 Hz (due to toggling) which is indeed sufficient. Additionally, this lab also included building a scanning module such that a scanning signal be displayed at ~2 Hz on internal LEDs when buttons on a keypad are pressed. The idea is that each row runs at a slightly different offset clock cycle and pressing all 4 columns in conjunction results in a synchronized blinking. Furthermore, the designs of both took advantage of the physics for PNP and NPN transistors as to not drain more than 8 mA for the FPGA pins but get sufficient current draw.

I spent approximately 15 hours in this lab all together including familiarizing myself with the idea and a whole lot of debugging

Design and Testing Methodology

The overall logic was stored in four modules where the top module called on the counter, decoder, and scanner submodules. It also included the assign logic prescribing which digit to select (based on the count declared in the top module) and assigned LEDs to be the inverse of the column inputs in the scanner. On the topic of the scanner submodule, it contained a counter in it of itself (targetting that 2 Hz) and contained logic to scan a 4 x 4 keypad by setting a 4-bit row vector to exhibit HIGH across a bit, one at a time. As previously stated, the parameterized clock divider in the top module was 18 bits whereas here, it increased to 25 bits (lower frequency naturally).

Schematic

{#fig-schematic}

?@fig-schematic shows the electical layout of the design. Because driving every segment at 10 mA is strenuous (especially if we consider a case where both digits display 8), 150 \(\Omega\) was the appropriate value per the equation where \(R_{seg} = (3.3V-2)/10mA\) making 150 our ideal choice. Additionally, the transistors and the resistor values associated with the keypad scanner were also paramount. First, to explain, the display was multiplexed by having 2 independent 2N3906 PNP transistors control a common anode of each digit respectively (specifically the Collector pin of the transistor). These transistors are configured such that they saturate when the FPGA pin (connected to Base) reads LOW. The Emitter is simply connected to +3.3V. Another thing to clarify is the NPN setup for each row of the keypad (4 row inputs). Following the E155 site instructions, the Emitter was grounded to the FPGA ground, the Base was connected to an FPGA pin (row outputs) via a resistor (\(R_{base}\)). The Collector was wired to the rows of the keypad as shown in ?@fig-schematic and the columns were wired both to the FPGA as column inputs and to +3.3V via a 1 k\(\Omega\) pullup resistor. The value of this resistor is somewhat arbitrary and should be a decent value to pull-up. However, it does inform the value of \(R_{base}\) as stated earlier. The equation is practically \(R_{base} = (3.3V-0.7V)\) where 0.7V is the voltage drop across the resistor divided by \(I_{base}\) which we expect to be \(I_{collector}/10\) or 0.33mA. This ultimately yields a value of 7.8 k\(\Omega\) though a 2.2 k\(\Omega\) serves our purpose of saturating just fine.Results and DiscussionThe overall design and construction was successful as confirmed via simulation (incoming) and through the physical circuitry.
